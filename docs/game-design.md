# Game Design: Realms & Ruin

## Premise

The Under-Vaults of Kaz-Dahrum are the buried foundations of an ancient citadel. Their sealed levels contain a relic sought by a powerful patron, but the vaults are not inert ruins: old ward-engines still enforce impossible rules, rival expeditions compete for the same route, and something beneath the foundations is learning the party’s names.

The party is not only trying to survive. It is deciding what survival is worth.

## Session loop

Each expedition follows three acts:

1. **The Descent** — establish the contract, reveal the first dungeon law, and choose what supplies or people receive protection.
2. **The Fracture** — introduce a rival, injury, betrayal, or reality shift that makes the original plan unreliable.
3. **The Price** — force a final choice between completing the objective and preserving the crew, then resolve the dungeon’s response.

Every scene should answer three questions:

- What does the Job demand right now?
- Who or what does the Crew need?
- Does this location become more Ordered or more Chaotic?

## The Job and the Crew

Track two expedition pressures from 0–6:

- **Job:** progress toward the patron’s objective, treasure, maps, and reputation.
- **Crew:** trust, readiness, shared supplies, and the willingness to carry one another home.

The facilitator raises Job when the party advances the contract and raises Crew when the party protects people at a real cost. A failed scene may reduce either track. At the end of Act III, compare the tracks:

- **Job leads:** the contract is fulfilled, but the expedition’s human cost becomes the ending’s central question.
- **Crew leads:** the survivors leave with their bonds intact, while the unclaimed objective changes the wider world.
- **A tie:** the party gets what it came for, but the vault marks the crew for a future consequence.

These are pressures, not alignment points. Players can pursue either one for sincere, selfish, or conflicted reasons.

## Righteous and Ruthless approaches

When a character faces a consequential obstacle, the player may declare an approach before resolving it.

### Righteous

The character accepts a tactical loss to uphold an oath, protect a captive, spare a defeated foe, or share a scarce resource. The facilitator grants a durable relationship, clue, or future advantage, but introduces an immediate cost such as damage, lost time, noise, or a worsened position.

### Ruthless

The character chooses brutal efficiency, coercion, forbidden power, or betrayal. The facilitator grants immediate position, damage, speed, or information, but adds a mark of corruption: collateral harm, a new enemy, a tainted location, or a debt that will matter in Act III.

Neither approach is a morality score. The important rule is that the choice must alter the fiction and the tactical state immediately.

## Ordered and Chaotic dungeon states

The vault has a shared state: **Ordered**, **Balanced**, or **Chaotic**.

- **Ordered:** traps synchronize, wards obey exact conditions, enemies coordinate, and geometry is predictable. Clever planning is strong, but mistakes are punished precisely.
- **Balanced:** ordinary exploration rules apply; this is the default starting state.
- **Chaotic:** rooms mutate, wild magic leaks through, routes change, and enemies act unpredictably. Improvisation is strong, but reliable plans decay.

Each scene has one state trigger. For example, activating a ward may push the vault toward Ordered; using corruption or destroying a stabilizer may push it toward Chaotic. The state should change the next procedure, not merely the description.

## Encounter ingredients

The first scenario should include:

- one clockwork obstacle with a readable pattern;
- one rival adventuring group with a competing contract;
- one choice involving an injured or trapped companion;
- one room whose map changes when the state shifts;
- one eldritch presence that can be fought, bargained with, or redirected;
- one relic whose safe recovery conflicts with the fastest escape.

## Compatibility note

This project uses familiar concepts such as ability checks, armor, hit points, conditions, and d20 resolution as optional compatibility language. It does not reproduce or depend on any third-party rulebook text. The standalone rules will define their own procedures before any conversion notes are added.

## Art direction

The initial document should remain text-first. Future original art may explore either:

- warm tavern amber against dungeon violet with oil-painted texture and illuminated parchment; or
- stark black-and-white woodcut engraving with restrained blood-red or eerie-green accents.

The art direction is intentionally undecided until the rules have survived the first playtest.
